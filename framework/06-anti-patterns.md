# Chapter 6: Anti-Patterns We Reject

*The AI Threat Modeling Framework*

---

## Learning from failure

Anti-patterns are recurring failures we have seen in reviews, incidents, and postmortems. Naming them helps teams recognize the slide before they step on it.

Each anti-pattern includes: what it looks like, why it fails, what to do instead, and which principle it violates.

---

## The Model-in-a-Box

**Looks like:** Security review focuses on the foundation model's safety card, benchmark scores, and provider certifications—while ignoring tools, retrieval, memory, MCP servers, and production orchestration.

**Why it fails:** Real systems fail at integration boundaries. The model is rarely the only attacker interface.

**Instead:** End-to-end system analysis. Include every component that influences behavior or exercises authority.

**Violates:** Principles 1, 6 · Value 6 (model in isolation)

---

## Prompt-as-Policy

**Looks like:** "We told the model never to share secrets" is listed as the primary access control. Security sign-off references the system prompt.

**Why it fails:** Prompts instruct; they do not enforce. Adversarial input, tool chains, and model updates bypass verbal rules.

**Instead:** Deterministic policy gateways for sensitive operations. Treat prompts as defense in depth.

**Violates:** Principle 4 · Value 3 (deterministic boundaries)

---

## Diagram Freeze

**Looks like:** Threat model last updated at initial launch. Prompts, tools, retrieval corpora, and MCP configs changed dozen times since. Architecture diagram unchanged—so reviewers assume risk unchanged.

**Why it fails:** AI systems drift without code deploys. A frozen diagram is a false comfort.

**Instead:** Change-triggered refresh policy. Version linkage. Scheduled reassessment.

**Violates:** Principle 9 · Value 4 (continuous modeling)

---

## Human Rubber Stamp

**Looks like:** High-risk actions require "human approval," but reviewers see only a summary generated by the agent, have seconds to decide, lack context to assess risk, and cannot reverse the action.

**Why it fails:** Oversight without comprehension is theater. It shifts liability without reducing harm.

**Instead:** Meaningful control: sufficient context, time, expertise, and reversal capability—or automate with stricter bounds.

**Violates:** Principle 8 · Value 5 (meaningful human control)

---

## Single-Agent Blindness

**Looks like:** Each agent in a multi-agent workflow has an individual threat model. No one models delegation graphs, shared memory, handoff protocols, or emergent composition.

**Why it fails:** The workflow's power exceeds any single agent's review. Attackers chain capabilities.

**Instead:** Composition analysis. Workflow-level authority maps. Multi-agent-specific abuse cases.

**Violates:** Principle 6 · Value 7 (explicit trust boundaries)

---

## Benchmark Absolutism

**Looks like:** "We passed the industry safety benchmark" closes the security conversation. No mapping from benchmark scope to production threats.

**Why it fails:** Benchmarks measure what they measure. They rarely include your tools, your data, your adversaries, or your failure modes.

**Instead:** Adversarial evaluations derived from your threat scenarios. Honest uncertainty about gaps.

**Violates:** Principles 7, 11 · Value 2 (evidence over intention)

---

## Autonomy by Default

**Looks like:** Agent ships with broad production credentials. Restrictions added only after security review "finds time."

**Why it fails:** Attackers do not wait for your hardening sprint. Blast radius is maximal during the riskiest learning period.

**Instead:** Least privilege from day one. Earn autonomy with evidence. Expand deliberately.

**Violates:** Principle 7 · Value 9 (observable, reversible actions)

---

## Shadow AI

**Looks like:** A team adopts a tool, browser extension, consumer chat assistant, or locally installed MCP server without going through the documented review process—often to "just try something" or meet a deadline. It reaches production data or production credentials before anyone threat-models it.

**Why it fails:** An unreviewed component is an unreviewed trust boundary. It can read, retrieve, or act on the same data and credentials as reviewed components, but carries none of their controls, logging, or ownership. In MCP deployments this shows up as MCP09 Shadow server—a developer-installed stdio server that never appeared on the approved catalog—but the failure is general: **any unsanctioned AI component in the production trust zone**.

**Instead:** Maintain an approved catalog of tools, models, and MCP servers, with a fast, low-friction path to request additions—so the sanctioned path is also the fast path. Monitor for unapproved network egress and local server installation.

**Violates:** Principles 1, 9 · Value 6 (end-to-end system analysis)

---

## Eval-Then-Forget

**Looks like:** The team ran a red-team exercise or adversarial eval before launch. The score went in the slide deck. Eight months later the model, prompts, tools, and threat landscape have all changed—but nobody re-ran the tests. The threat-model document was updated; the *evidence behind it* was not.

**Why it fails:** Diagram Freeze is about stale architecture. Eval-Then-Forget is about stale **proof**. A current threat model with expired evidence is paperwork with a fresh date. Goodhart's law makes this worse when the original score became a marketing or procurement claim nobody wants to revisit.

**Instead:** Tie evaluations to versioned manifests (Pattern 4, Pattern 11). Define refresh triggers that re-run scenario-based tests—not only when the diagram changes. Treat eval results like certificates: useful, dated, scoped.

**Violates:** Principles 7, 9, 11 · Anti-pattern cousin: Benchmark Absolutism

---

## Kitchen-Sink Context

**Looks like:** One agent is given every tool, every document collection, and every permission "just in case"—because splitting into focused agents feels like overhead, or because nobody mapped which capabilities each task actually needs.

**Why it fails:** You have maximized two risks at once: the **instruction-injection surface** (every document and tool description is a channel) and the **blast radius** (one compromised session can reach everything). Least privilege applies to context and tools, not only to API scopes.

**Instead:** Task-scoped agents or dynamic tool allowlists. Grant retrieval access per workflow, not per platform. Review composition (Principle 6) with the question: "What does this agent need for *this* job—and nothing else?"

**Violates:** Principles 3, 5, 6 · Pattern avoided: Agent-action inventory with justified scope

---

## Compliance Theatre

**Looks like:** Threat model exists because policy requires a document. Findings do not change architecture, controls, tests, or monitoring. Same risks appear in every release review—unchallenged.

**Why it fails:** The artifact became the goal. The system did not improve.

**Instead:** Connect every material threat to a decision and verified control. Measure changed decisions, not page count.

**Violates:** Principle 12 · Value 2 (evidence and action)

---

## Happy-Path Intent

**Looks like:** Personas include only the ideal customer. Abuse cases assume external hackers, not insiders, confused users, or manipulated context. No consideration of affected non-users.

**Why it fails:** Most real harm is messier than the OWASP diagram suggests.

**Instead:** Abuse-case workshops with diverse participants. Model misuse, failure, and indirect harm.

**Violates:** Principle 10 · Value 8 (abuse cases and affected humans)

---

## Unowned Residual Risk

**Looks like:** Threat model lists "residual risk: medium" with no owner, no acceptance record, no review date, and no trigger for reassessment.

**Why it fails:** Unowned risk becomes everyone’s problem and no one’s responsibility—until an incident assigns blame.

**Instead:** Explicit disposition: mitigate, avoid, transfer, accept with accountable owner, or investigate. Date the decision.

**Violates:** Principles 11, 12

---

## Anti-pattern quick reference

| Anti-pattern | One-line symptom | First fix |
|--------------|------------------|-----------|
| Model-in-a-Box | Only the LLM was reviewed | Map the full system |
| Prompt-as-Policy | System prompt = access control | External policy enforcement |
| Diagram Freeze | TM older than prompts/tools | Define refresh triggers |
| Human Rubber Stamp | Approvals without context | Redesign oversight or automate |
| Single-Agent Blindness | Per-agent reviews only | Model the workflow graph |
| Benchmark Absolutism | "We passed the benchmark" | Scenario-based adversarial eval |
| Autonomy by Default | Prod credentials on day one | Least privilege + evidence tiers |
| Shadow AI | Unapproved tool or MCP server in production | Approved catalog + egress monitoring |
| Eval-Then-Forget | Pre-launch eval never repeated | Version-linked scenario retests |
| Kitchen-Sink Context | One agent with all tools and data | Task-scoped agents and allowlists |
| Compliance Theatre | PDF changes, system doesn't | Tie threats to decisions |
| Happy-Path Intent | One friendly persona | Abuse-case workshop |
| Unowned Residual Risk | Risk with no owner | Named acceptance + review date |
